DSC 106 · Project 2

Persuasive and deceptive visualization

Raynard Joseph Taneka · rtaneka@ucsd.edu

Proposition

Abortion clinic access improved in the United States from 2017 to 2020.

I used the same state-level dataset to argue both for and against this statement. In both charts, the change in the number of clinics is used as a rough measure of access.

Supporting the proposition

More clinics opened in 15 states

Design decisions and rationale

  1. Show only states with an increase −1.5

    I filtered out states that lost clinics or did not change. This makes the positive result easier to see, but it is also the most deceptive choice because readers do not see most of the country. I considered showing every state, but that would weaken the argument this chart is trying to make.

  2. Sort states by their increase +1.5

    I put the largest increase at the top so comparisons can be made using position and bar length on a common scale. Alphabetical order was another option, but it made the main pattern harder to notice.

  3. Use green bars 0

    Green is commonly connected with growth, so it helps the increases feel positive. The color does not change the values, but it does influence the mood of the chart. I kept all bars one color so the display would stay easy to read.

  4. Lead with the total gain −0.5

    The subtitle says that 42 locations were added before a reader reaches the small note explaining that only gain states are shown. The number is correct, but the order gives the good news more attention than the limitation.

Opposing the proposition

The gains did not offset the losses

Design decisions and rationale

  1. Combine all state gains and losses +1.5

    I grouped the state changes into clinics added, clinics lost, and the national net change. This includes every state and makes the overall result easy to compare. A state-by-state chart would provide more detail, but the national pattern would take longer to find.

  2. Use a shared zero line +2

    All three bars use the same scale and meet at zero, so their direction and size are not distorted. This is an earnest choice because readers can compare the 42 gained with the 47 lost without a truncated axis.

  3. Use red for the losses 0

    The red bars direct attention toward decline and support the opposing argument. The green bar still represents gains, so both directions remain visible. I used a darker red for the net result to make the final outcome stand out.

  4. State the conclusion in the title −0.5

    The title tells readers that the country ended with fewer clinics instead of asking a neutral question. It is supported by the data, but it frames the chart negatively before the reader looks at the values.

Which side I lean toward

I lean against the proposition. The supporting chart shows real growth in some states, but it leaves out every state that stayed the same or lost clinics. When all states are included, the country had five fewer clinics overall, and more states lost clinics than gained them. Clinic count is still only a rough measure of access because it does not show travel distance, appointment availability, state population, or how many patients each clinic can serve.

Final reflection

The straightforward part was calculating the state changes and building bars on a common scale. The difficult part was deciding how much context each chart should include. I was surprised that I could make the same data feel positive just by filtering to the 15 states with gains. Nothing in that chart is invented, but leaving out the losses changes the conclusion a reader is likely to remember.

I now think ethical visualization means being honest not only about individual values, but also about the context needed to understand them. Color, sorting, and a persuasive title can be acceptable when the chart still lets a reader check the claim. Filtering becomes misleading when the missing data would reverse the overall conclusion and the omission is hard to notice. The small note on the supporting chart makes the choice technically visible, but it does not receive the same attention as the headline.

Neither chart proves whether practical access improved. Both rely on clinic count as a proxy, and the opposing chart also hides differences between individual states by reducing them to a national total. A stronger analysis would add population, distance, clinic capacity, and policy information. This project showed me that a clear chart can still be incomplete, so the data transformation and its limits are part of the argument, not just technical details.

Data and method

Source: Guttmacher Institute Abortion Data by U.S. State, provided through DSC 106. I used the field Change in the no. of abortion clinics, 2017–2020. The supporting chart filters for positive values and sorts the states by change. The opposing chart sums all positive and negative values and reports their net change.
